## The game

On November 4, 2026, developer Shikhar released Sumiro — an ink-wash samurai adventure that runs in the browser for free. A wanderer in a straw hat crosses misty cliffs, torii gates and stone stairways; climbing, riding lifts, working through chapters, with a 30–40 minute playtime. The launch thread passed 2.6 million views in two days.

![Sumiro's title screen: ink-wash style, the samurai standing on water](/showcase/sumiro-title.jpg)

![Gameplay demo excerpt (from the 97-second original), the project's best-looking part](/showcase/sumiro-demo.gif)

His own line was: "Anyone can make games now." The Chinese write-up we're drawing from spends its length on what that line leaves out — the actual 24 hours, and the conditions behind them.

## How the 24 hours were laid out

Six steps, six off-the-shelf tools (Opus 5.5, Flux 3 Max, GPT Image, Tripo, Blender, Three.js). Each step feeds the next:

1. **Start with a video, not code.** Flux 3 Max generates a short reference clip — this video is the project's art bible. World, character, palette, camera: everything later derives from it, following OdinLovis's Video2Game approach.
   ![The output of step 1: the reference clip every later scene is screenshotted from](/showcase/sumiro-step1-reference.jpg)
2. **Break the video into scenes.** Screenshots become assets rebuilt in Blender and placed into Three.js. The easy-to-miss detail: dynamic workflows inside Claude Code, with multiple agents building and checking each other in parallel — "a group of agents working separately, verifying each other," not one agent end to end.
   ![Step 2's result: waterfall, torii and stone stairways, all rebuilt from video screenshots](/showcase/sumiro-step2-scenes.jpg)
3. **Build the character.** Four side views generated with GPT Image from a screenshot, Tripo turns them into a 3D model, Opus adds cloth physics and animation.
   ![How four side views become a moving 3D character (sped up)](/showcase/sumiro-step3-character.gif)
4. **Play it first.** The whole step is one sentence: play the prototype before adding more, confirm it actually runs. The only move in the pipeline that runs against AI instinct.
5. **Let agents fill the levels.** GPT Image generates concept art for each level in the locked art style, up to eight images per level; each concept becomes one playable 3D level.
   ![Step 5: level concept art generated by GPT Image in one locked style — one image per level](/showcase/sumiro-step5-concepts.jpg)
6. **Loop.** Play, report bugs, let agents fix, play again.
   ![From concept image to a runnable, jumpable level (sped up)](/showcase/sumiro-step6-level.gif)

## What the hype line leaves out

The article insists on two preconditions before repeating "anyone can make games now":

**24 hours is a third-game number, not a first-game number.** Before Sumiro, Shikhar had already shipped Spiderbench (a browser Spider-Man game — that one took three days plus a 5x Claude subscription) and Walden, a katana duel he calls his first prototype. The hard-won lessons — aligning Blender assets with Three.js, keeping parallel agents from fighting, cloth physics not clipping — were all paid before this 24-hour clock started.

**Two parts the pipeline doesn't cover.**

> Music is the one unsolved part: models can't hear, so a separate music-generation toolchain is required — the seventh step outside the six.

The other gap is controls: the demo video looks stiff because it was agent-operated and script-recorded. A human hands-on plays much better — but that isn't the part the 24 hours produced.

## The two places copying it actually breaks

* **Judging the reference video.** Anyone can open the tools. The hard part is knowing whether the video is good enough — whether the style can carry a level, whether the character's silhouette is readable, whether the palette turns muddy in 3D. Pick wrong here and the other five steps are wasted.
* **Step 4 is human-only.** AI never tires of adding more — assets, concept art, levels. But playing the prototype before continuing is a judgment only a person can make; the author made it its own step because he had paid for skipping it.

## Verdict

> **Read it as a compression story, not a democratization one.**
> The scary number is real — 24 hours, six tools, one playable game — but it belongs to someone who had already built two games and stepped in every pit first. The tools raise the floor very high; the ceiling stays with whoever keeps practicing. Of the six steps, step 1's judgment is the one that can't be copied; the rest can be followed as written.
